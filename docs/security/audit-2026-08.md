## Disclosure and limitations

This audit was performed by an AI model, not by a professional security firm. It
is an internal review intended to raise the floor, not an attestation. Treat its
conclusions accordingly:

- It is a **code review**, not a penetration test: no dynamic instrumentation of
  a running device, no camera/QR fuzzing, no side-channel measurement, no
  adversarial tooling was run against a live build.
- **Absence of a finding is not evidence of absence.** An AI reviewer can miss
  bugs a human specialist would catch, and vice versa. The cryptographic
  primitives themselves live in the Rust core (`rrn-crypto`/`rrn-identity`,
  reached over the uniffi FFI) and were audited in the `station` repository's
  companion report; this review covers the *mobile* layers — key custody on the
  device, the transport and pairing envelope as the phone builds them, the QR
  ceremony surfaces, background execution, and platform configuration.
- Findings were verified against the code at the commit above; each carries a
  concrete failure scenario rather than a hypothetical.
- A professional audit is still warranted before any deployment where real
  people depend on this software's guarantees — particularly given the
  privacy-sensitive user population the project's own framing implies.

This report mirrors the `station` repo's `docs/security/audit-2026-08.md` in
format and severity scale, for consistency. Where a mobile finding is the client
half of a server-side finding already recorded there, it is cross-referenced by
its `RRN-A-###` id.

## Scope and methodology

Reviewed in full: `src/crypto` (SecureStore/keychain custody, seal/open,
SignedPayload, the hand-rolled base64/hex codecs, the CBOR value model,
constants), `src/wallet` (Wallet lifecycle — create/load/change-passphrase/
export/factory-reset, the record builders that sign proposals/vouches/listings/
disputes/governance, held shards, recovery ceremony), `src/network` (StationClient
envelope build/seal/verify, Pairing handshake and SAS, paired-station and
endpoint resolution, per-station nonce serialization, background credential and
background-sync drain, mDNS discovery), `src/diagnostics` (crash log ring and
report formatting), and the QR intake paths (`QRScanner`, `addressQr`,
`recoveryShard`, `recoveryCeremony`). Reviewed by targeted inspection:
onboarding/passphrase policy, the recovery and help-recover screens, the export
and factory-reset screens, clipboard sinks, and the Android/iOS platform
configuration (`AndroidManifest.xml`, `network_security_config.xml`,
`build.gradle`, `Info.plist`, `MainActivity`). Cross-cutting checks: every
`kind`-discriminated signed record for domain separation, all deserialization of
scanned/received bytes, secret-material flow across the FFI boundary, secure-store
gating per entry, `yarn npm audit`. The `station` repo's `docs/threat-model.md`
(mobile sections) was checked against the code it describes.

Severity scale: **High** (practical compromise of funds, keys, or identity),
**Medium** (compromise requiring social engineering, unusual position, or
combination with another flaw; or a protocol property weaker than documented),
**Low** (hardening gaps, defense-in-depth), **Info** (observations,
accepted-risk confirmations).

## Summary

No High-severity findings. The mobile client is disciplined about the things
that matter most: the wallet secret never crosses the FFI boundary into JS,
signing and sealing are delegated to the one audited Rust path, the unlocked
wallet is held only in memory and dropped whenever the app leaves the
foreground, every signed record carries a domain-separating `kind`, the
canonical-CBOR envelope binds its `recipient` inside the signed bytes, transport
nonces are serialized through an in-process lock and persisted before send, and
scanned QR bytes are validated defensively before ever reaching the FFI. The
findings below are concentrated at the *ceremony and exposure* level — what the
pairing and recovery flows assume about the human in front of the phone, and
what leaves the device through platform channels (clipboard, screen, keychain
accessibility).

## Findings

### RRN-M-001 · Medium — Pairing SAS is 32 bits and grindable by a MitM

**Where:** `src/network/Pairing.ts` (`confirmationCode`, `SAS_HEX_LEN = 8`),
`src/screens/join/Pair.tsx` (the confirm step), and the pairing-TOFU section of
`station/docs/threat-model.md`.

Pairing is the trust root of the whole design (ADR-0008): there is no
certificate to pin, so a man-in-the-middle present at first contact is caught
*only* by the human comparing an out-of-band short authentication string. That
SAS is `blake3(tag ‖ station_pk ‖ mobile_pk)` truncated to **8 hex characters —
32 bits**. The threat model asserts a MitM "would have to present its own key,
which changes the code, so the comparison catches it." But in an interposition
the attacker generates *both* interposed keypairs (the station key it shows the
mobile, and the mobile key it shows the operator), and can therefore search for
a pair whose two codes collide.

**Failure scenario.** An attacker on the pairing LAN interposes between phone
and station. It must make the mobile-side code `blake3(tag ‖ A_station_pk ‖
mobile_pk)[:8]` equal the operator-side code `blake3(tag ‖ station_pk ‖
A_mobile_pk)[:8]`, where it controls `A_station_pk` and `A_mobile_pk`. It
generates ~2¹⁶ candidate station keys (recording each mobile-side code) and ~2¹⁶
candidate mobile keys (recording each operator-side code); by the birthday
bound a collision in a 32-bit space is likely across those two sets. Both are
just Ed25519 keypair generations plus a blake3 hash — seconds to minutes on a
laptop. The human then compares two codes that *match*, and pairs with the
attacker, who now holds an authenticated channel to the real station on the
victim's behalf and can read/relay every sealed request.

**What holds today.** The attack requires physical/network presence at pairing
(the same precondition the TOFU risk already names) and does not help against a
station the user has *already* paired. The SAS still stops a passive or naive
MitM that reuses one key. It is the *grinding* margin that 32 bits fails to
provide.

**Recommendation.** Lengthen the SAS to at least 12 hex (48 bits; the station's
`paired::confirmation_code` and this file's `SAS_HEX_LEN` must move together and
stay byte-identical), and preferably render it as grouped decimal/words so users
actually compare all of it. Update the threat model's pairing-TOFU section to
state the SAS's bit length and the grinding bound explicitly, rather than
implying any key change is detectable. A commit-based SAS (each side commits to
its key before the codes are revealed) would remove the grind entirely and is
worth an ADR if pairing is ever hardened further.

### RRN-M-002 · Medium — Help-recover authenticates nothing about the requester

**Where:** `src/screens/main/HelpRecover.tsx` (the `confirm` step),
`src/wallet/recoveryCeremony.ts` (`parseRecoveryRequest`), `src/crypto/ffi.ts`
(`RecoveryRequestInfo` exposes only `targetAddress`).

This is the mobile realization of **RRN-A-001** (station: "recovery ceremony
requests are unauthenticated"). A recovery request is, by necessity, not
authenticated to any requester — the legitimate owner has lost their key — so
the *only* defense the design can offer is procedural, and the mobile confirm
screen is exactly where that defense has to live. It is absent. When a holder
scans a `rrnrecover-req:` QR, the confirm card shows only the **target address**
(whose identity is being recovered) and "You hold a K-of-N piece." It does not
show any fingerprint of the requester's ephemeral recovery public key, and
carries no warning to verify — out of band — that the person asking is really
the owner. `RecoveryRequestInfo` does not even surface the recovery pubkey for
the UI to display.

**Failure scenario.** An attacker who knows a victim's holder set (holder
addresses are stored as plaintext recovery metadata) approaches two holders
separately — "Aaron lost his phone, he asked me to collect his recovery, here's
the code" — showing each a request minted under the attacker's own ephemeral
key. Each holder scans, sees the correct *target* address (which the attacker
knows and put in the request), unlocks, and contributes. Two holders standing
next to each other have no way to notice they were shown *different* ceremonies,
because nothing on screen reflects the requester's key. With `K` contributions
the attacker reconstructs the victim's identity.

**Recommendation.** Implement the mobile half of RRN-A-001's recommendation #2:
have the FFI surface a short fingerprint of the request's `recovery_pubkey`,
display it prominently on the confirm card, and add normative warning copy
("Only contribute if you have confirmed, in person or by a channel you already
trust, that this person is <target> and is really recovering their own key. A
recovery request is exactly as trustworthy as the person showing it to you.").
The fingerprint lets two holders cross-check that they are in the *same*
ceremony. Track this against the recovery-ceremony threat-model section that
RRN-A-001 asks the station repo to add.

### RRN-M-003 · Low — Wallet export and addresses go to the shared clipboard; no secure screen flag

**Where:** `src/screens/main/ExportWallet.tsx:113`,
`src/screens/main/Receive.tsx:60`, `src/screens/main/TransactionDetail.tsx:280`,
`src/screens/main/Diagnostics.tsx`, `src/components/ErrorBoundary.tsx:138`;
`android/app/src/main/AndroidManifest.xml` and `MainActivity.kt` (no
`FLAG_SECURE`); `ios` (no screenshot/recording guard).

Export produces the full `.rrnwallet` blob as base64 and copies it to the
**system clipboard** (`Clipboard.setString(exported)`). The system clipboard is
readable by every other app on Android, is surfaced in clipboard history/
suggestions, and on iOS is mirrored to other signed-in devices via Universal
Clipboard. Addresses and formatted crash reports use the same sink. Separately,
no window in the app sets Android `FLAG_SECURE` (or an iOS screenshot/recording
guard), so the passphrase entry field, the pairing SAS, the recovery request/
response QRs, and the export blob are all capturable by screenshots, screen
recording, and the app-switcher thumbnail.

The exported blob is still argon2id+AEAD ciphertext, so clipboard capture alone
does not yield the key — but it hands an attacker the exact ciphertext to mount
an offline passphrase guess against (compounding RRN-M-004), and the export
screen's own banner already tells users the file plus their passphrase "is you."

**Recommendation.** For the wallet export specifically, prefer a
direct-transfer path (QR or share-sheet to a chosen target) over the global
clipboard; at minimum, auto-clear the clipboard after a short delay
(`Clipboard.setString('')`) and label the entry sensitive where the OS supports
it (Android 13+ `ClipDescription` sensitive flag). Set `FLAG_SECURE` on the
Android window (and the iOS equivalent) at least for the passphrase, export,
SAS, and recovery-QR screens. Confirm the app-switcher snapshot is masked.

### RRN-M-004 · Low — Passphrase policy admits low-entropy secrets

**Where:** `src/screens/onboarding/passphraseStrength.ts`
(`MIN_PASSPHRASE_LENGTH = 12`, `estimateStrength`).

The only enforced gate is a 12-character minimum; the strength meter is
explicitly advisory (its own header says "not a substitute for the real thing")
and does no dictionary or repetition analysis, so `"aaaaaaaaaaaa"` or
`"password1234"` passes the gate and proceeds. The passphrase is the *outer*
protection on the wallet, and the wallet ciphertext is not confined to the
device — it can be exported to the clipboard (RRN-M-003), and the station-side
backup archive is passphrase-derived too — so a weak passphrase is directly
attackable offline once any copy of the ciphertext leaks. argon2id (in the Rust
core) raises the per-guess cost, but does not rescue a passphrase drawn from a
tiny space.

**Recommendation.** Keep argon2id as the per-guess brake, but strengthen the
policy: reject the lowest strength bucket rather than only warning, add a cheap
repetition/common-password check, and raise the practical floor (a longer
minimum, or a passphrase-vs-random-words nudge). Document, next to the export
and backup features, that passphrase strength is what stands between a leaked
`.rrnwallet` and the identity.

### RRN-M-005 · Low — No device-integrity posture on a sideloaded key custodian

**Where:** whole app; `SIDELOAD.md` distribution model.

The app is distributed as a sideloaded APK (no store review) and is the
authoritative custodian of the identity key. It performs no root/jailbreak
detection, no emulator check, and no basic tamper/repackage check. On a rooted
device, the keychain/keystore protection SecureStore relies on is substantially
weaker, and a repackaged build could alter the pairing-confirm or recovery-
confirm flows that RRN-M-001/002 identify as the human trust points. This is a
defense-in-depth gap, not a discrete exploit, and root detection is itself
bypassable — but its total absence is worth a deliberate decision rather than an
omission.

**Recommendation.** Decide and document the posture. At minimum, a soft
root/emulator signal surfaced to the user (not a hard block), and — since the
build is signed with a known release key (keystore per T1.11.4) — a self
signature check to detect repackaging. If the decision is to rely purely on the
OS keystore and the passphrase layer, record that in the mobile threat-model
section so the pilot community is told what a rooted phone does and does not
protect.

### RRN-M-006 · Low — Release APK ships without ProGuard/R8

**Where:** `android/app/build.gradle:60`
(`def enableProguardInReleaseBuilds = false`, applied at `minifyEnabled`).

Release builds run with minification and shrinking off, so the distributed APK
keeps full Java/Kotlin symbol names and structure (the JS runs as Hermes
bytecode, which is already compiled, but the native/Java layer is in the clear).
This lowers the effort to reverse-engineer the client and locate the pairing/
recovery/keychain code paths, and forgoes the dead-code stripping that shrinks
attack surface. It is a hardening gap, not a vulnerability.

**Recommendation.** Enable R8 for release (`enableProguardInReleaseBuilds =
true`) and validate the existing `proguard-rules.pro` against a real
pair/send/recover run — React Native + Nitro modules need keep rules, so this
needs a device pass, not just a compile. Fold into the same release-hardening
milestone as RRN-M-005.

### RRN-M-007 · Info — Background credential and its secret co-located at device-unlock

`src/network/backgroundCredential.ts` stores `BG_SYNC_BLOB` (a full-power
signing wallet, re-encrypted under a random secret) *and* `BG_SYNC_SECRET` (that
secret) side by side at `WHEN_UNLOCKED` accessibility with **no biometric gate**,
so a background task can reconstruct a signing wallet without a passphrase. This
is a real weakening — anyone able to read the keychain while the device is
unlocked obtains a signing identity — but it is **default-off, opt-in, device-
bound (`THIS_DEVICE_ONLY`), cleared on background-sync-off / last-unpair /
factory reset, and already documented as an accepted residual risk** in the
station threat model (T1.3.6, "Background-sync signing credential"). Recorded
here to confirm the mobile implementation matches the documented bargain, and to
carry forward the threat model's own suggested fix: a station-issued
*read-scoped* background token minted at pairing, which would remove the need to
park a full signing key at rest. The random secret is drawn from a throwaway
keypair's public-key bytes (≈252 bits, unpredictable) — an unconventional but
sound entropy source, the same one the pairing token uses.

### RRN-M-008 · Info — Mobile reads any member's ledger view by address

`StationClient.balance(address?)`, `.transactions(address)`, and
`.nextNonce(address)` pass an arbitrary `address` through to the station. This
is the client half of **RRN-A-004** (the station does not scope these read
methods to the authenticated signer). Nothing on the mobile side is wrong — the
scoping decision belongs on the station — but it is noted so the two halves are
tracked together: if the station adopts read-scoping, these signatures can drop
the `address` parameter for everything except a member's own lookups.

### RRN-M-009 · Info — Reply bound to request by nonce only

`StationClient.openReply` verifies the reply is sealed to this wallet, signed by
the paired station's key, carries the expected envelope version, and echoes the
exact nonce that was sent — but it does not bind the reply to a hash of the
*request* method/params. This is the client half of **RRN-A-010** and is safe
under the same conditions: transport nonces are strictly increasing per mobile
and persisted before send (`stationNonce.ts`, serialized through `withLock`), so
a reply can only satisfy the request whose nonce it names. Two invariants keep
it safe and should stay true: the nonce counter must never reset without
re-pairing, and the request-side timestamp/skew discipline must remain tight. A
future refactor could bind the reply to `blake3(request payload)` for negligible
cost.

### RRN-M-010 · Info — Smaller observations

- **Clipboard for crash reports** (`Diagnostics.tsx`, `ErrorBoundary.tsx`):
  crash reports are copied to the clipboard for the user to paste back. They
  carry JS stacks and messages (never the secret key — that stays in Rust) but
  can include addresses or memo text that surfaced in an error. Low stakes;
  worth a line in the diagnostics copy that a report may contain identifiers.
- **mDNS/manual station input is untrusted by design** (`Discovery.ts`): a
  discovered or hand-typed station is a *claim*; `validate()` rejects malformed
  advertisements but authenticates nothing, and pairing is the only trust
  boundary. Verified correct and consistent with ADR-0008 — no action, recorded
  so it is not re-derived.
- **Hand-rolled base64/hex codecs** (`crypto/base64.ts`, `crypto/hex.ts`): both
  validate strictly (length/alphabet), throw on malformed input, and exist to
  avoid engine-dependent `atob`/`btoa`. Reviewed for round-trip and rejection
  correctness — sound. They handle encoding only, never key material.
- **QR intake is defensive** (`addressQr.ts`, `recoveryShard.ts`,
  `recoveryCeremony.ts`): every scanned string is prefix-checked, base64 is
  decoded in a `try/catch`, the nickname hint is url-decoded/trimmed/clamped to
  200 chars, and structural validation is delegated to the Rust FFI. No
  injection sink was found on any scan path.
- **`AppState` lock drop** (`WalletSession.tsx`): the unlocked wallet is dropped
  on any non-`active` state (`inactive` and `background`), so it is cleared even
  for the iOS app-switcher/notification-shade transition, not only a full
  background. Correct and slightly stronger than the comment implies.
- **`usesCleartextTraffic` / plain HTTP is intentional** and now explicit in
  `res/xml/network_security_config.xml` with a rationale; iOS mirrors it with
  `NSAllowsArbitraryLoads=false` + `NSAllowsLocalNetworking=true`. The sealed
  envelope is the security boundary, not TLS. Verified — no action.

## What was checked and found sound

Recorded so the next reviewer does not re-derive it:

- **Key custody.** The wallet secret never crosses the FFI into JS; `Wallet`
  holds only an opaque handle, and signing/sealing/opening/recovery all delegate
  to the Rust core. The unlocked wallet lives only in memory and is dropped on
  every non-foreground `AppState` transition. Factory reset erases every
  wallet-scoped keychain entry (`Object.values(SecureStoreKeys)`), including the
  background credential.
- **At-rest protection.** Each secret is stored under its own keychain
  `service`, `WHEN_UNLOCKED_THIS_DEVICE_ONLY` on iOS (no iCloud sync/migration),
  hardware-backed preferred on Android with a software fallback, biometric-gated
  when the user opts in. Non-secret records (paired stations, nonces, cursors,
  held ciphertext shards) are deliberately un-gated with a stated rationale each.
- **Transport envelope.** The request envelope is canonical dCBOR built by the
  shared value model, binds `recipient` *inside* the signed bytes (so it cannot
  be peeled and re-sealed to another station), is signed then sealed to the
  station key; the reply is opened with the wallet key, verified against the
  station key, version-checked, and nonce-matched before use.
- **Replay protection.** Per-station request nonces are strictly monotonic,
  reserved and persisted *before* send, and every mutation runs through an
  in-process serialization lock (`stationNonce.withLock`) so overlapping callers
  (a background poll racing a user send) can never be handed the same value.
- **Signed records.** Every record the phone signs (proposal, confirmation,
  vouch, listing/update/close, inquiry, contract, dispute/response/verdict/
  escalation/ballot, cosign, vote, charter signature) embeds a domain-separating
  `kind` (`rrn.tx.proposal`, `rrn.dispute.verdict`, …), so a signature for one
  record type cannot be replayed as another; amounts are integer centicommons
  and the CBOR model rejects floats at construction.
- **Recovery shard custody.** Held shards are stored exactly as scanned —
  ciphertext sealed to the holder, each below the reconstruction threshold — and
  the raw Shamir share is never in the clear on the contribute path (re-sealed to
  the requester's ephemeral key inside Rust). The *authentication* gap on that
  path is RRN-M-002; the *confidentiality* of a single held shard is sound.
- **Dependencies.** `yarn npm audit` reports one moderate advisory, `eslint`
  `8.57.1` (deprecation/unsupported), which is a devDependency not shipped in the
  app. No runtime-dependency advisory outstanding.

## Suggested order of remediation

1. RRN-M-002 recovery-key fingerprint + warning copy, and RRN-M-001 SAS length
   — the two human-trust surfaces; both are small and close real gaps (RRN-M-001
   requires a coordinated one-line constant change on the station side).
2. RRN-M-003 clipboard/secure-screen handling for the export and passphrase/
   SAS/QR screens — small, self-contained UI/platform changes.
3. RRN-M-004 passphrase policy — enforce above the lowest bucket; pairs with
   RRN-M-003 to close the offline-guess exposure.
4. RRN-M-005 device-integrity posture and RRN-M-006 R8 — a single release-
   hardening pass, decision-first for RRN-M-005.
5. RRN-M-008 tracks with the station's RRN-A-004 read-scoping decision; RRN-M-007
   tracks with the station-issued read-scoped background token.
